🔬 Research

  • Vals AI, "Do agent teams pay off?" (Vibe Code Bench: 50 full-stack web apps from specs, graded by UI tests; GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5; single agent vs a lead with up to five subagents; medium and max effort; via TLDR) — teams cost 1.8 to 5.1× a single agent. The only significant team gain was Sol at medium effort, +7.3 points (77.6% to 84.9%); the other three team deltas ran −0.3 to +3.4 and weren't significant. Raising Sol's single agent to max effort added 11.4 points (to 89.0%) for 1.6× the medium team's cost. For Opus, max effort didn't help: the single agent scored 91.5% at $4.08 an app at medium against 89.8% at $23.77 at max, and the max-effort team's 93.2% cost about $122 an app and wasn't significant; Opus teams ran up to 2.3× slower because the lead wrote a contract and delegated in waves. Flags: one run per setup, one benchmark, two different harnesses, and the team runs carried a delegation prompt the singles didn't. This is route-compute's second and fourth forks measured: "a chain is one agent even when asked to parallelise" and "effort before tier" both held, and Opus 5.5 at medium was the best value on the table. A row for the evidence file. ↗
  • Harvard working paper (Fiona Chen and James Stratton): "AI coding agents generate more code, but not more software" (Ars Technica Oct 12; HN 344 / 287) — Jellyfish engineering telemetry paired with GitHub across more than 700 software companies, 2021 to March 2026, about 300 million work events. After agent adoption, per-worker output rose about 30% in lines, 20% in commits and 23% in pull requests; the resolution rate of Jira issues and epics showed no statistically significant change; review time from PR to merge rose about 49% and the share of PRs with changes requested nearly doubled. The authors' reading: the coding gain is absorbed downstream, at review. Flags: "no significant change" is not zero, and it's a firm-level average. The Oct 11 Cockroach "hospital" is the design that answers it: the agents review too, with a human precedent log. ↗
  • "AI models can provably hide arbitrary capabilities" (Draguns, Motwani, Douglas, Schroeder de Witt; arXiv 2610.13928; NeurIPS 2026; HN 226 / 112) — an encrypted neural network hidden inside a host model's weights, decrypted and run only on a secret trigger, and argued under cryptographic assumptions to be hard to elicit or interpret even with full access to the weights; on a small chemistry proxy task the hidden capability survived fine-tuning, weight noising, gradient-based trigger search and latent adversarial training; models released for defence research. Flags: a proof of concept on one small task. Why it's here: every open-weight shadow candidate this digest lists is a weight file, and "bench on your own board items" tests behaviour, not what's dormant in it. ↗
  • Anthropic's Frontier Red Team: "AI models may be capable of self-replicating without access to a datacenter" (Oct 12; Reuters, Al Jazeera) — Gemma4-26B-A4B run distributed across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes bought for about $55 each, at 0.39 tokens a second; five models tested on setting up inference and spreading across a firewalled fleet; GLM-5.2 was the only open-weight model to complete end-to-end replication, reaching all eight contained nodes in four of five trials; Qwen3.5-9B set up inference on eight of nine hardware types, its success rising from 45% to 61–84% as it inherited notes across generations; the extrapolation is "by end of 2026" at historical rates. Flags: the team says its evaluation "keeps the cyber-exploit element of the replication trivial", so the headline is "an upper bound on real-world success", and no replicator exists. ↗ · Reuters
